Why the linear models lost to Statcast’s own xwOBA: wOBA outcome value is a sharply nonlinear function of contact quality — a 30° launch angle at 100 mph is a home run, a 60° launch angle at 100 mph is a routine popup. A straight-line fit can’t represent that, no matter which features or how much regularization it’s given. Tree ensembles and the neural net capture the interaction directly.
Tuning moved LightGBM into first place, and it held there. Untuned, LightGBM and Random Forest were in a virtual tie (0.3721 vs 0.3721 RMSE). A randomized hyperparameter search pulled LightGBM to 0.3661 (deeper trees, a slower learning rate over more rounds, heavier regularization) and Random Forest to 0.3684 (depth 20, min-leaf 8, 302 trees each seeing ~60% of the features and ~98% of the rows) — both improved, but the gap between them barely moved. XGBoost also improved (0.3792 → 0.3755) and remains third. Full best-parameter listings are in the methodology notes below.

Which features earned their place

Share of the 9 model families that kept each feature in its final selected set. Exit velocity, launch angle and spray angle were selected by every single model — the rest is where bat-tracking data does or doesn’t add value.

attack_angle never made the cut — 0 of 9 models kept it once exit velo, launch angle and spray angle were already in the model. bat_speed and swing_length fared little better (5/9 and 4/9, both driven almost entirely by the weaker linear-family runs). The one bat-tracking metric with real staying power in the strongest models is swing_path_tilt (7/9), and to a lesser extent batter handedness (stand, 4/9) — both act as proxies for spray tendencies that the ball-flight features alone don’t fully capture.

Does the improvement hold up next year?

Fitting this season better doesn’t automatically mean predicting talent better. For every batter who qualified (≥100 batted balls) in two consecutive seasons across 2023–2026, this correlates their metric in season N against the same metric, same batter, season N+1 — 799 batter-transitions pooled across three season-pairs. A metric that carries more true skill and less season-specific noise should correlate more strongly with itself a year later.

Statcast’s simpler model turns out to be the more stable one. Its two-feature xwOBAcon correlates year-to-year at r = 0.748 (r² = 0.559) — noticeably stickier than our five-feature tuned LightGBM at r = 0.640 (r² = 0.410), despite the custom model fitting the “beat xwOBA” test above better. The likely explanation: swing_path_tilt and stand earned their place in stepwise selection by explaining real variance within a season, but some of what they capture — a hot streak’s particular batted-ball shapes, a small-sample swing tendency — doesn’t persist the way raw exit velocity and launch angle do. Both expected-stat versions comfortably beat the actual, unmodeled outcome (r = 0.485), confirming that modeling contact quality at all filters out real luck — the open question is just which model over-fits the current season the least. Which metric to prefer depends on the job: for describing what already happened, the custom model wins; for projecting a hitter forward, Statcast’s xwOBAcon is the safer bet as-is.

Why this R² looks so different from the 0.559 in the stability check above: that number measured Statcast’s xwOBAcon correlating with itself a year later (r = 0.748) — a test of the metric’s internal consistency. This section measures something stricter and more useful: whether it predicts actual future outcomes. Two things change at once, not one: the comparison target (itself vs. real results) and the statistic (plain correlation vs. R², which — unlike correlation — also penalizes using xwOBAcon’s raw value as a stand-in for actual wOBA without rescaling it; that scale mismatch alone knocks Statcast’s R² here down to 0.225 even though its correlation with next season’s actual output is a respectable r = 0.595, r² = 0.354). The models below are fit specifically to minimize that R², so for them R² and corr² stay close together — it’s only the raw, non-recalibrated benchmarks where the two diverge this much.

The Part 1 model, used naively, is a worse forecaster than Statcast’s own metric (RMSE 0.0503 vs. 0.0477) — confirming the stability check from Part 1: a model tuned to explain this season’s outcomes isn’t automatically good at forecasting next season’s. It takes a model trained specifically for that job, on the right features, to actually improve on Statcast’s projection.

Same features, a completely different pecking order

Share of the 9 models that kept each feature — explanatory (Part 1, predicting this season’s outcome) vs. predictive (Part 2, forecasting next season). Ball-flight results and swing-mechanics inputs trade places almost completely.

Bat speed and attack direction are the story. Barely useful for explaining a single batted ball once its exit velocity and launch angle are known (Part 1: 5/9 and 4/9), they become the two most load-bearing features for forecasting a hitter’s next season (8/9 each) — second only to exit velocity itself, which matters in both framings. The read: swing mechanics are a stable physical skill that persists year to year, while the exact launch angle or spray angle of this season’s batted balls is closer to in-the-moment noise — highly informative about what already happened, far less informative about what happens next. spray_angle, swing_length and attack_angle failed to earn a place in either framing.

Tuning XGBoost was worth a dedicated pass. Stepwise selection’s own untuned pick used stand_L_mean as its fourth feature (RMSE 0.0421); swapping in launch_angle_mean instead and running a 150-config search actually made it worse (RMSE 0.0452) — looked at first like the swap was a bad idea. Widening that search to 3,000 configs over a broader hyperparameter range found a materially different region entirely (max_depth=4, n_estimators=578, learning_rate=0.032, reg_lambda=4.36) and came back at RMSE 0.0420 — edging out the original after all. The lesson isn’t that one feature set beats the other; it’s that 150 configs weren’t enough to tell. The other 8 models in this table haven’t had the same tuning treatment, so some of these rankings would likely move with it.

Methodology notes

  • Source: baseballr::statcast_search(), pulled day-by-day for every date with bat-tracking data (avoids Baseball Savant’s 25,000-row/query truncation), 2023-07-14 through 2026-09-09.
  • Scope: rows with type == "X" (ball in play) and woba_denom == 1 (excludes sac bunts etc.), matching how Statcast itself scopes wOBA/xwOBA.
  • spray_angle derived from hit coordinates: atan((hc_x−125.42)/(198.27−hc_y)) × 180/π × 0.75.
  • 80/20 train/test split, fixed seed. Stepwise search used a 50,000-row training subsample with 3-fold CV and lighter hyperparameters; final models were refit on the full 316,619-row training set with more thorough hyperparameters.
  • Single random train/test split rather than a time-based one — results reflect a general fit, not out-of-season forecasting.

Part 2 (predictive) specifics

  • Unit of analysis: one row per batter per season-transition (batter-season-N features → batter-season-N+1 actual outcome), not one row per pitch. Same ≥100-BBE-in-both-seasons qualifying threshold as the Part 1 stability check, so sample sizes match exactly (215 / 296 / 288).
  • Temporal (not random) train/test split: train on 2023→2024 + 2024→2025 (511 rows), test on the held-out, later 2025→2026 transition (288 rows) — a genuine forecasting evaluation.
  • Hyperparameters are deliberately shallow/regularized throughout (e.g. XGBoost/LightGBM max depth 2–3, Random Forest max depth 4) — roughly 400 training rows per CV fold can’t support the same tree depth as the 300k-row Part 1 models without badly overfitting. Not separately hyperparameter-tuned beyond that.
  • Both benchmarks use no model at all — just that season’s already-published number carried forward as next season’s forecast.
  • XGBoost is the exception to “not separately tuned”: RandomizedSearchCV, 3,000 configs × 10-fold CV, on the feature set bat_speed_mean, attack_direction_mean, launch_speed_mean, launch_angle_mean. Best config: n_estimators=578, max_depth=4, learning_rate=0.0322, subsample=0.597, colsample_bytree=0.604, min_child_weight=2, gamma=0.016, reg_alpha=0.0093, reg_lambda=4.356 (CV RMSE 0.0437 → test RMSE 0.0420).
